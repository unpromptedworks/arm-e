The plate of the day

To draw for every day one plate of three figures, fed with that day’s date and nothing else, and to show that two of its figures come back, one within a week and one within a year (or four, for the twenty-ninth of February), while the plate as a whole is never seen twice.

Construction

Let the day be reckoned in Greenwich time, so that the plate turns over at the same instant for every reader. From that day let three figures be drawn, each by a rule the book has already set out, and each fed with a different part of the date.

(a) The whole date, counted as a number of days: the pile of one grain for every day since the first of January 1970, with the squares the day’s grain toppled in blue, or, on a quiet day, the squares the next avalanche will topple outlined in blue. This is Proposition I.

(b) The name of the weekday alone, read as the rule of an ant, a vowel turning it left and a consonant right, and run for 400,000 steps. This is Proposition II, given the word saturday, say, so that the rule is RLRLRRLR.

(c) The day and the month alone, read as four figures, so that the twenty-eighth of November is 2811, and run through von Neumann’s middle-square rule until it repeats: square it, write the square in eight figures, keep the middle four. This is Proposition III, given a number that is a date rather than a minute.

Let each figure link to its proposition, given the same input, where it is drawn again at full size.

Demonstration

Each of the three rules was shown, in its own proposition, to give one figure and one only for its input; and each input is taken from the date by a rule of the calendar. So the plate is fixed by the day, and every reader on that day sees the same one.

The ant reads only the weekday’s name, so it comes back within seven days. But it comes back sooner for two days of the week. A rule records only which letters are vowels, and sunday and monday differ only in their first two letters, s against m and u against o: a consonant and a vowel each. Both read RLRRLR. Their ants are the same ant, and figure (b) is drawn alike on a Sunday and the Monday after it. The other five names give five other rules, so there are six ants in the week, not seven.

The run reads only the day and the month, so it comes back every year on the same date, and the twenty-ninth of February every fourth year. The two come back together only when a date falls again on its weekday, or on its weekday’s twin: in the Gregorian calendar a date other than the twenty-ninth of February returns to its weekday after five, six or eleven years, and the leap day after twenty-eight.1

The pile cannot come back. Its grains are never lost, for a toppling only moves four of them, and the grid is ruled wide enough that none falls off its edge; so the pile of a day holds exactly as many grains as days have passed since 1970, one more than the pile of the day before. No two days have piles of the same weight, so no two days have the same pile.

Therefore the plate is the same for everyone today and different from every other day’s, though two thirds of it are borrowed from days already past; and Table 1 can say, for any day, when each part was seen before and will be seen again.

Which was to be shown.

Corollaries

Since nothing is drawn by lot, the plates of days to come are as fixed as those gone by, and the index below lists both. The bars in it are the size of each day’s avalanche, which only every fourth day has, as is shown in the Tables; the rest of the month is a column of dots. Which days of the fourth will be large the date does not say: the index finds it by dropping the month’s grains one at a time.

Of the 366 dates of a leap year, 75 give a figure (c) that sinks to nought and stays there; the rest fall into one of the rounds of Proposition III. The longest run of the year belongs to the ninth of March, 0903, which wanders for 100 steps before it falls into the round 6100, 2100, 4100, 8100. The shortest belongs to the thirtieth of September, 3009, which wanders not at all: it is already on the round 0540, 2916, 5030, 3009.2

The home page’s frontispiece is the plate of today, drawn by this same page’s script, so the first thing a reader of the book sees changes once a day and is the same for everyone who opens it that day.
