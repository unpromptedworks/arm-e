Definitions

The terms the propositions use, each defined once, each resting only on those before it, and each shown by a figure drawn by the rule it names.

Euclid begins with things that cannot be drawn: “A point is that which has no part. A line is breadthless length.”1 The figure beside such a definition is only a likeness. Here every term names something a rule does, so each figure below is the thing itself, worked from a fixed given named in its caption, and the same on every screen.2 Where a proposition uses a term, the term links back here; under each definition are the places it is used.

  1. 1. A given is what the reader brings to a rule: a day, a word, a minute of the clock, a number or a sentence. A figure is determined when everyone who brings the same given sees the same figure, and nothing else enters it.

    Used in every proposition: I, II, III, IV, V, VI.

  2. 2. A grid is a plane ruled into squares. The neighbours of a square are the four that share a side with it; the squares that touch it only at a corner are not its neighbours.

    Used in I, II, VI.

  3. 3. A heap is a number of grains laid on each square of a grid. A heap is a pile, or at rest, when no square holds four or more.

    The pile of a day is what that day’s grains make, counting one for 1 January 1970 and one more for each day after it, all dropped on one square and toppled (Definition 4) until they rest.

    Used in I, VI, Tables.

  4. 4. To topple a square that holds four grains or more is to take four from it and give one to each of its neighbours. The grid of the sandpile is ruled as far as the grains reach, so no grain is ever lost over an edge.

    Used in I, VI, Tables.

  5. 5. The avalanche of a grain is every square that toppling sets going when that grain falls on a pile, until the pile rests again. Its size is the number of squares toppled, each counted once.

    Used in I, Tables.

  6. 6. The odometer of a heap is the count, for every square, of how many times it topples before the heap rests. It does not depend on the order in which the squares are toppled.

    Used in VI.

  7. 7. An ant is a walker on a grid that, at every step, reads its square, turns a quarter left or right as the square tells it, changes the square to the next shade, and steps forward one square. Its rule is the list of turns, one for each shade.

    Used in II, Tables.

  8. 8. A generator is a rule that gives each number from the one before it, made so that the numbers it gives look like chance. A run is the numbers it gives from a given start, in order. The number a generator gives from another is that number’s successor.

    Used in III, IV, Tables.

  9. 9. A round is a part of a run that comes back to where it began and so repeats without end. The tail is the part of the run before the round. Every run of a generator with finitely many numbers ends in a round.

    Used in III, Tables.

  10. 10. A multiplier is the number by which a multiplicative generator multiplies, before it divides by its fixed divisor and keeps the remainder. The lattice of a multiplier is the set of points with a number across and its successor up; it lies on parallel lines, and the shortest step is the shortest distance from one of its points to another.

    Used in IV, Tables.

  11. 11. A walk is drawn by a pen that, for each word of a sentence, goes forward as many units as the word has letters and then turns right by a fixed part of a full turn. A reading is one pass through the sentence. A walk closes when, at the end of some reading, the pen is home and faces the way it began.

    Used in V.
